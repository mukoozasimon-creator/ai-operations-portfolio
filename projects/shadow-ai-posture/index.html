Shadow AI is an asset problem wearing a policy costume

What is shadow AI?

Most organisations respond to unsanctioned AI with an email. An email does not produce an inventory, a score, or a trend line. This framework treats shadow AI the way a mature programme treats any other unmanaged asset: discover it, characterise it, rate it, dispose of it, and measure whether the population is getting safer.

It is any AI tool people use for work that nobody officially approved. Someone signs up with their own email, pastes in something they should not, and now company information sits on a computer you do not control and cannot get it back from. Most companies react by sending a warning email. An email does not tell you what is being used, how risky it is, or whether things are improving. This page does.

Scope note. This framework covers AI that people in your organisation use and AI they wire into things. It does not cover assurance of AI systems you build and sell to customers. That is a larger programme, and ISO/IEC 42001 clauses 4 through 10 are the right spine for it. This is the discovery and control layer that has to exist first.
What this does not cover. This is about AI your people use. It is not about AI products your company builds and sells. That is a much bigger job with its own rulebook.
The danger is not what the AI can see. It is what the AI can do. A spreadsheet macro someone wrote without asking has a fixed blast radius. An agent with an OAuth token, a write scope and a schedule does not. That is why this model scores autonomy and reach as its own dimension, weighted equally with the sensitivity of the data.
The danger is not what the AI can see. It is what the AI can do. A chatbot you type into can only leak what you tell it. An AI that has been given the keys to your email, your files or your customer records can change things, send things and spend money on its own, while nobody is watching. So this framework scores what a tool can do just as heavily as what it can see.

Why the app list is where an attacker looks first

Why this list matters more than the others

A consent grant is not a login. When a user approves a third-party app, the platform issues that app an access token and a refresh token. From that point the holder does not need the password and is never asked for the MFA code, because nobody is signing in. That is why a grant is worth more to an attacker than a stolen credential: it is durable, it is quiet, and it was authorised by a real employee. Whether disabling the account also kills the grant depends on the platform and how it is configured. Do not assume it does. Test it on one account before you rely on it.

When someone clicks Allow on an outside app, that app is handed its own key. It does not use the person's password after that, and it is never asked for a code from their phone, because it is not logging in as them. That is why these are worth more to an attacker than a stolen password. The key keeps working, it is quiet, and a real employee approved it. Changing the password does not take the key back. Somebody has to go and cancel it. And do not assume that switching off the person's account cancels it either. That depends on the system. Test it on one account and find out.

The names are boring on purpose. Meeting Scheduler. PDF Utility. Notes Sync. A low-traffic app with read scopes trips no brute-force rule, no impossible-travel rule and no anomalous-login alert, which is exactly the point. And a grant outlives its reason: somebody connects a tool for a two-week project, finishes, and forgets, while the scopes stay live for years. The same holds for grants you approved yourself. A legitimate vendor with wide access to your tenant is a legitimate route in for whoever breaches that vendor.

The names are dull on purpose. Meeting Scheduler. PDF Utility. Notes Sync. A quiet app that only reads things sets off none of the alarms that catch someone guessing passwords, and that is the point. What a bad one looks like

What a bad one looks like

  • Sort every grant list by last used, first. Anything unused for 90 days is either dead weight or a foothold. Both need the same action, so you do not have to tell them apart to act.
  • Scopes wider than the app's stated job. A scheduling tool asking to read mail is not a misconfiguration, it is a claim you should make it prove.
  • One user, recently granted, unverified publisher. Consent phishing lands on individuals, not fleets. A single grant nobody else in the organisation has is the shape to look for.
  • A publisher name that does not match the app name. Or a verified-publisher field that is simply blank.
  • A grant that survived offboarding. Cross-check the grant list against your leaver list. A live grant on a disabled account is the finding that funds the programme.
  • Sort the list by when each app was last used. Anything untouched for three months is either forgotten or is somebody quietly keeping a way in. You do not need to work out which, because the answer is the same either way.
  • An app asking for more than its job needs. A calendar tool that also wants to read your email is not a mistake. Make it explain itself.
  • One person, added recently, from a company you cannot verify. These scams target one person at a time. An app nobody else has is the one to look at.
  • The company name does not match the app name. Or there is no company name at all.
  • Somebody left and their app is still connected. Compare the list against people who have left. A live connection on a closed account is the thing that gets your boss to take this seriously.

Bands and override flags

What the score means

Arithmetic is a floor, not a verdict. Five conditions override the computed band, because each one represents a failure the weighted average is too polite about.
Sometimes the sums are too kind. Five things are bad enough to change the answer no matter what the score works out to.
  • Secrets or credentials observed in prompts, so Critical. The exposure already happened. Rotation, not review.
  • Regulated data plus a personal account, so Critical. You have regulated data in a place you cannot subpoena, revoke or audit.
  • No named owner and write access, so escalate one band. Something can change your systems and nobody has agreed to answer for it.
  • Vendor trains on inputs with no verified opt-out, so escalate one band. Your inputs are now somebody else's training corpus, permanently.
  • A standing grant or API key that survives a credential reset, so escalate one band. Resetting the password and re-enrolling MFA does not touch an issued token. Only revoking the grant does, and nobody does that at offboarding unless a process makes them.
  • Somebody pasted in a password or a key. Straight to the worst level. It has already leaked. Change the password today.
  • Private details about real people, on somebody's personal account. Straight to the worst level. You cannot get that back and you cannot prove what happened to it.
  • It can change things and nobody is responsible for it. One level worse. Something can act on its own and there is nobody to ask about it.
  • The company behind it learns from whatever you type. One level worse. What went in is now part of their system forever.
  • It holds a key that still works after the password is changed. One level worse. Changing the password does not lock it out. Somebody has to go and take the key back, and almost nobody remembers to.

Nothing escalates past Critical. The first two set the band outright, whatever the score. The override set moved from four conditions to five when the standing-grant condition was added; scores recorded under the earlier set are unaffected, because that condition is off unless you tick it.

Nothing goes past the worst level. The first two set it outright, whatever the score. There used to be four of these and now there are five. Any score you wrote down before still stands, because the new one is off until you tick it.

Before you revoke anything

Before you switch anything off

A revoked grant does not warn anyone. It stops working, silently, and the first person to notice is often a customer. Mass revocation is the fastest way to turn a security finding into an outage, and an outage is how a programme loses the mandate it just earned. Contain in this order.

When you cut off an app, nothing announces it. It just stops working, and the first person to notice is often a customer. Switching off everything at once is the quickest way to turn a good piece of work into a disaster your team gets blamed for. Do it in this order.

  1. Find what depends on it. A grant is often the only thing moving data between two systems nobody thinks of as connected. Check the last-used timestamp and the call volume before you touch it.
  2. Name the owner and give them the date. If no owner exists, finding one is the first task, not a reason to wait.
  3. Kill the token, not just the login. Disabling the account may leave the grant live. Revoke the grant itself, and rotate any API key that went with it.
  4. Stage it. Revoke the worst one, wait a working day, then the next. One failure is diagnosable. Forty at once is an incident with your name on it.
  5. Close the door behind you. If nothing stops the same user re-approving the same app tomorrow, you have bought a week. Route high-risk scopes to an admin approval instead of an open consent prompt.
  6. Write down what broke. That list is the business case for the consent gate, and it is the only version of that argument anyone believes.
  1. Find out what relies on it. These apps are often the only thing quietly passing information between two systems nobody realised were joined up. Check when it was last used before you touch it.
  2. Name the person responsible and tell them the date. If nobody owns it, finding someone is the first job, not a reason to put it off.
  3. Cancel the key, not just the login. Closing someone's account may leave the app's key working. Cancel the app's access itself, and change any password or key that came with it.
  4. Do them one at a time. Cut off the worst one, wait a day, then do the next. If one thing breaks you can fix it. If forty break at once, that is now your problem.
  5. Shut the door behind you. If the same person can just say yes to the same app again tomorrow, you have bought yourself a week. Make the risky ones need an admin to approve them.
  6. Write down what broke. That list is how you convince your company to fix this properly, and it is the only version of the argument anyone actually believes.

Incident response annex

If something has already gone wrong

A Critical row's disposition says to open an incident record. This is what that record should actually contain, and the one step people skip: evidence comes before remediation. Rotating a key before you have captured what it touched destroys the thing you would have used to answer "what actually left."

The worst-level decision above tells you to write down what happened. This is what to actually write, and the one step people skip: capture the evidence before you fix anything. Changing the password before you have written down what it had access to destroys the only record of what actually happened.

Classify it first, in one of three ways. Confirmed: you can show what left, and when. Suspected: the evidence points to exposure but you cannot yet show it directly. Near miss: caught before anything left. This is a separate axis from the tool's Low to Critical band; a Low-scored tool can still produce a Confirmed incident, and the classification, not the standing band, decides how you respond right now.

Work out which of three this is, first. Confirmed: you can actually show what got out, and when. Suspected: it looks bad but you cannot prove it yet. Near miss: caught before anything left. This is different from the tool's usual score. Even a tool you scored as fine can still cause a Confirmed incident, and it is this classification, not the old score, that decides what you do right now.

  1. Confirm scope before you touch anything. What left, when, and who had access. A timestamp range beats a guess. This is the record; write it down before the next step overwrites the evidence.
  2. Preserve, then contain. Export or screenshot the logs, grants and messages you are about to revoke. Only then work through the revoke sequence above. Order matters once, and this is the once.
  3. Notify by who needs to act, not by who might be upset. Legal or your data protection lead if personal data was involved, the business owner named on the row, and leadership if the classification is Confirmed. Silence is a choice with its own consequences.
  4. Record the classification, the timeline and the root cause on the row itself, alongside the enforcement date. A Critical row that closes with no incident note looks identical to one that was never actually reviewed.
  5. Decide if the score should change. An incident is often evidence for an override flag you had not ticked, secrets observed, or a standing grant you did not know survived. Update the row rather than filing the incident somewhere the register cannot see it.
  1. Work out what actually happened before you touch anything. What left, when, and who could get to it. An actual time range beats a guess. Write this down before the next step wipes the evidence.
  2. Save the evidence, then lock things down. Screenshot or export the logs and access you are about to switch off. Only then do the steps above. This is the one time order really matters.
  3. Tell the people who need to act, not just the people who will be upset. Your legal or privacy person if anyone's personal details were involved, the person responsible for that tool, and your boss if you are sure something actually got out.
  4. Write down what it was, when it happened, and why on the tool's row, next to the date you fixed it. A worst-level row closed with no explanation looks exactly the same as one nobody actually checked.
  5. Ask whether the score should change. An incident is often proof of something you had not ticked yet, like a key that still worked after a password reset. Update the row instead of writing the incident down somewhere the list will never see it.

Closing a row

Ticking a tool off the list

A decision that is never applied is indistinguishable from no decision at all, so the register carries three fields that turn a disposition into work somebody can be held to. replacement names the sanctioned thing people go to instead. review_due is the date this row is looked at again; the band suggests the interval, annually down to thirty days. enforced is the date the decision was actually applied, and until it is filled the row is decided but not done.

A decision nobody carries out is the same as no decision, so every tool on the list has three extra boxes that turn a decision into real work. One says what people should use instead. One says when you will look at it again. One says the day you actually did it. Until that last one is filled in, you decided and then did not do it, and the list will keep saying so.

One rule is enforced rather than suggested: a High or Critical row cannot be marked done without a named replacement. Blocking with nothing to offer does not remove the demand that created the tool, it relocates it somewhere you cannot see. If most of your High rows have that field empty, the honest finding is not that people misbehaved, it is that the organisation has a capability gap, and the register now counts it for you.

One rule is not a suggestion: you cannot tick off a bad one until you name the safe thing people should use instead. Taking a tool away does not remove the reason people wanted it, it just moves it somewhere you cannot see. If you keep leaving that box empty, the honest finding is not that people misbehaved, it is that your company has not given them a good enough tool. The list counts that for you now.

RUN

Run it in thirty days

A first pass that fits around an existing job. It produces a register, a scored population, decisions on the worst rows, and a baseline you can re-run. It does not produce a finished programme, and saying so out loud is what makes the plan credible.

A first go that fits around a normal job. At the end you have a list, a score for each thing on it, decisions on the worst ones, and a starting number to improve on. It will not be finished, and saying so is what makes the plan believable.

Week 1

Get the signals

Go looking

  • Pull 30 days of DNS or proxy logs against the dictionary query.
  • Export third-party app grants from your identity provider and major SaaS tenants.
  • Ask finance for 90 days of card and reimbursement lines matching AI vendor names.
  • Send the amnesty survey. Say plainly that nobody gets in trouble, and mean it.
  • Search a month of web records using the query from step 1.
  • Open the settings page of your big work tools and list the connected apps.
  • Ask finance for three months of card payments to AI companies.
  • Ask everyone what they use, and promise nobody gets in trouble. Then keep that promise.
Week 2

Build the register

Make the list

  • Collapse signals into one row per tool. Expect fewer rows and more users than you assumed.
  • Assign an owner to every row. Leave genuinely unowned rows blank; they score worse for it.
  • Walk the admin console of your five largest platforms for AI features already switched on.
  • Turn everything you found into one line per tool. There will be fewer tools and more people than you expected.
  • Put a name against each one. If nobody owns it, leave it blank on purpose. That makes the score worse, which is correct.
  • Check your five biggest work tools for AI features somebody already switched on.
Week 3

Score and decide

Score it and decide

  • Score every row on the five dimensions. Score what you observed, not what policy allows.
  • Apply the override flags. Anything Critical goes to containment the same day.
  • Give every High row a disposition, an owner and a 30-day date.
  • Answer the five questions for each tool. Answer honestly about what is happening, not what is allowed.
  • Anything that lands on the worst level gets dealt with that same day.
  • Everything on the next level down gets a decision, a person and a date one month out.
Week 4

Baseline and report

Write it up

  • Run the maturity assessment. That number is your baseline, and it is allowed to be low.
  • Report three things: population, the worst five rows with decisions, and the capability gap driving people off-platform.
  • Diarise the re-run for 90 days out. A framework nobody re-runs is a document.
  • Answer the thirteen questions. Whatever number you get is your starting point. It is allowed to be low.
  • Tell your boss three things: how many tools there are, the worst five and what you decided, and what people actually needed that made them go elsewhere.
  • Put a date in the calendar to do it all again in three months. A plan nobody repeats is just a document.
